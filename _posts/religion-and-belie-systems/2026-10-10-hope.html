Sometimes the smallest encounters leave the biggest footprints on our hearts.

This is a story about a little boy, a stranger in a hoodie, and the power we all carry to make tomorrow a little brighter.

The Boy Who Saw a Superhero

There I was, walking along a quiet road in Portugal, minding my own business.

Sunglasses on. Hoodie on. The sun doing its usual Portuguese thing: shining as if someone had forgotten to turn down the thermostat.

At sixty-two, I still go to the gym. I like to think I am in reasonably good shape. My shoulders agree. My knees, particularly on stairs, occasionally submit a formal complaint.

A few children were playing in the street. Among them was a little boy, perhaps seven years old, with sun-tanned skin, dark hair and enormous brown eyes.

He stopped. Looked at me. Looked again.

Then he walked towards me, wearing the peculiar expression of someone who has just spotted a dinosaur outside the bakery.

He lifted his right arm and gently touched my chest, where the familiar superhero emblem sat on my hoodie.

“SUPERMAN!” he cried, his whole face lighting up.

I smiled. For a moment, I saw myself at his age: the same wide eyes, the same imagination, the same certainty that heroes could make the world right.

“Yes,” I answered. “It’s hope.”

He frowned. “No! It’s SUPERMAN!”

There are arguments one simply cannot win with a seven-year-old.

“Tell me,” I asked, “what is Superman’s greatest power?”

“HE CAN FLY!”

“True.”

“AND HE’S REALLY STRONG!”

“Also true.”

“AND HE SHOOTS LASERS FROM HIS EYES!”

“Yes. Though I wouldn't recommend trying that in school.”

He laughed.

“But what if,” I asked, “his greatest power is none of those things?”

He stared at me. Now I had his attention.

We sat down on one of the simple concrete blocks that serve as benches around town. An older boy and a younger one, separated by more than half a century, united by a symbol on a hoodie.

I told him a story.

But perhaps it is better if you hear what happened next from him.

The Next Day, at School

The following morning, during break...

“YOU GUYS! YOU GUYS!”

I ran across the playground towards Miguel, Tomás and João.

Miguel was eating a sandwich. Tomás had a football. João was doing what João usually does: absolutely nothing useful.

“Guess what happened yesterday!”

“What?”

“I MET SUPERMAN!”

Tomás stopped kicking the ball. Miguel stopped chewing. Even João looked awake.

“Did he fly?”

“No.”

“Did he lift a car?”

“No.”

“Did he shoot lasers?”

“NO! Will you let me tell the story?”

“Then how do you know it was Superman?”

“Because he had the symbol on his hoodie. And he told me something about Superman that none of you know.”

That got their attention. We sat down.

Two Young Men and a World That Needed Hope

“Superman was invented a long, long time ago,” I began.

“When dinosaurs were alive?” asked João.

“No, João. In the 1930s.”

“Same thing,” he said.

“Two young men, Jerry Siegel and Joe Shuster, created him. Superman first appeared in a comic called Action Comics in 1938.”

“And the man said those were difficult times. There was the Great Depression. Many families struggled to find work or enough money to live. In Europe, hatred and dictatorship were growing. People were frightened about the future.”

“The two young men could not fly. They could not stop wars with their hands. But they could imagine a hero who stood up for ordinary people, especially people being pushed around.”

“At first Superman wasn't even flying the way he does now. He could leap huge distances. Imagine that! A superhero who started with giant jumps!”

I jumped up to demonstrate, caught my shoe on the bench and nearly landed in Miguel's sandwich.

“Careful,” said Miguel. “My lunch isn't a supervillain.”

“The important thing,” I continued, “wasn't how high he jumped. It was who he jumped in to help.”

“When people felt small and powerless, Superman gave them a picture of someone who would stand up for them.”

A Baby from the Stars

“Then he told me Superman's other name: Kal-El.”

“Kal-who?” said Tomás.

“Kal-El! He came from a planet called Krypton. His parents, Jor-El and Lara, knew their world was dying. They couldn't save everyone, but they sent their baby away in a little spaceship.”

For a moment nobody spoke.

“All alone?” Miguel asked.

“All alone.”

I imagined a baby crossing the stars, without anyone to tuck him into bed or hold him when he cried.

“But on Earth he was found by Jonathan and Martha Kent, who raised him as their son, Clark. They taught him to be honest, kind and brave.”

“And that is the strange thing,” I said. “His amazing strength came from being Kryptonian. But the lessons that made him a good man came from the people who loved him.”

Tomás thought about this. “So his mum and dad gave him his best powers?”

“In a way, yes.”

What Does the S Really Mean?

“Then the man pointed to the symbol on his chest.”

“In some newer Superman stories, that famous S isn't just the first letter of Superman. It's the crest of his family, the House of El, and it stands for hope.”

“HOPE?” said João. “That's not a superpower!”

“That's exactly what I said!”

“The man explained that the Hebrew word El means ‘God’. Some readers see religious echoes in the name Kal-El. People sometimes suggest meanings like ‘voice of God’, but that isn't a proven translation or a confirmed intention of the creators. Kryptonian is a fictional language, after all.”

“Still,” I said, “he told me the name and the story reminded him of someone else.”

“Who?”

“Jesus.”

Miguel blinked. “Jesus? As in church?”

“Yes. He told me he wasn't saying Superman and Jesus are the same. Superman is a made-up character. Jesus is central to his faith, and a historical figure whose life changed the world.”

“But the stories made him think about the same important question: If you have the power to help, what do you do with it?”

A Cat, a Bully and a Very Important Choice

“Superman can stop trains, rescue people from fires and, yes, save a cat stuck in a tree.”

João burst out laughing. “He can lift mountains and he rescues CATS?”

“Exactly! He doesn't say, ‘Sorry, kitten, I'm only available for world-ending emergencies.’”

“The man said that Jesus taught love for neighbours, care for the vulnerable and mercy. Superman stories often show a hero trying to protect life, even when doing so is difficult.”

“Superman is famous for his reluctance to kill, although different comics and films have portrayed that rule differently. What matters to the man is the ideal: great strength should be guided by compassion and restraint.”

“Think of the bigger boys who sometimes push the little children around,” I said. “If we help the smaller ones, or fetch a teacher, aren't we doing something brave?”

“Even if we can't fly?” Miguel asked.

“Especially then.”

“I'd still like a cape,” said João.

“Me too,” I admitted.

The Greatest Power

“And then the man asked me a question I couldn't stop thinking about.”

“Suppose Superman woke up tomorrow with no super-strength. No flying. No heat vision. Nothing. Would he still be Superman?”

“No,” João said. “He'd just be a man wearing his pyjamas outside.”

“That's what I thought. But the man said Clark Kent could still help someone who had fallen. He could still tell the truth. He could still stand beside someone who was scared.”

“He could still make someone feel less alone.”

“And then I understood.”

I looked at my friends.

“Superman's greatest power is HOPE.”

“Because when people see him coming, they believe that maybe everything isn't lost. That maybe someone cares. That maybe tomorrow can be better.”

“And you don't need to come from another planet to give somebody that feeling.”

“Maybe Superman is my dad when he works hard for us. Maybe it's my mum when she hugs me after a bad day. Maybe it's a teacher who doesn't give up on a child. Maybe it's a stranger who stops to listen.”

“Maybe,” I said, remembering the sunglasses and that dark hoodie, “it's an older man sitting on a concrete bench, talking to a little boy as though the boy's questions matter.”

Miguel was quiet for a moment.

“Can we be superheroes too?”

I smiled. “I think that's what he was trying to tell me.”

Then the bell rang. João tied his jacket around his shoulders like a cape, raised one arm and shouted, “UP, UP AND AWAY!”

He promptly tripped over his shoelace.

We helped him up, laughing together.

And perhaps that was our first little act of heroism.

Back on the Concrete Bench

I don't know what that little boy will remember of our conversation.

Perhaps he'll forget my face. Perhaps he'll forget the sunglasses, the dark hoodie, or the old fellow who still goes to the gym and occasionally believes he's twenty-five.

Perhaps he'll remember only that someone took time to sit beside him.

But I hope he carries one idea into the years ahead: you don't need a cape to be a hero, and you don't need superpowers to give someone hope.

At seven, I too believed in heroes. At sixty-two, I still do. Only now I know they often look like ordinary people.

They look like parents, teachers, neighbours, volunteers, strangers who stop to help, and children who without their parents knowledge deposit money into their accounts.

Maybe the world is not saved only by grand gestures. Maybe it is also saved, a little at a time, by small acts of kindness.

Final Thoughts — Faith, Hope and Tomorrow

We live in troubled times. We see the suffering of families caught in wars, from Ukraine to the Middle East, and elsewhere across the world. We see division, anger and fear. Some days the news feels so heavy that it's difficult to imagine a better tomorrow.

And yet, I refuse to believe that hope has become old-fashioned.

For me, Jesus offers something no comic-book hero can: the living foundation of my faith, and the call to love even when love is difficult. Superman, by contrast, is a fictional hero — but a wonderful reminder, especially to the young and the young at heart, that strength should protect, not oppress.

I do not confuse the two. I simply see a beautiful common lesson in the way I read their stories: power is worth little without goodness; courage is worth more when it serves others; and hope is something we can give away without ever running out.

Whatever our beliefs, surely we can agree that the world needs more hands reaching out and fewer fists raised in anger. More forgiveness. More compassion. More people willing to make life a little easier for someone else.

Faith can move mountains. Hope gives us the courage to face them. And love helps us move them together.

So the next time a little boy points at a superhero symbol, don't rush to tell him heroes aren't real.

Perhaps he's looking for one.

Or perhaps, with a little encouragement, he's becoming one.

Because as long as there is hope, there is a way.

And tomorrow? Tomorrow can still be beautiful.

With love, faith, and just a little bit of Superman.
