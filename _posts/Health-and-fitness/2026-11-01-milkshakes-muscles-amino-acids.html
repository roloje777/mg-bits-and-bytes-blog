Another Saturday Morning at Snow Haven, Welkom

There are certain sounds you never forget: the clink of a spoon against a milkshake glass, the hiss from a burger grill, and John announcing that he has discovered the secret to becoming Arnold Schwarzenegger.

It was another warm Saturday in the 1980s at Snow Haven, our favourite ice-cream-and-burger stop in the Hoefie — Welkom’s horseshoe-shaped shopping heart. John and I had barely settled into our seats when he leaned over the table with the confidence of a man who had read half a bodybuilding magazine.

John: “I've worked it out. To build muscles, you just eat more protein. Steak for breakfast, chicken for lunch, and perhaps a cow for supper.”
Me: “And what do you do when the cow objects?”

Before John could answer, Joseph appeared beside our table. And, as on our previous visit, he had somehow ordered a breakfast that looked like a complete training programme: oats, three poached eggs, and a generous chicken breast.

Joseph: “Morning, gentlemen. I heard the word protein. That usually means somebody is about to make a very expensive mistake.”
John: “So you're saying my cow idea won't work?”
Joseph: “I'm saying the cow is innocent. Pull up a chair. We need to talk about what protein actually does.”

It's Not Just Protein — It's the Building Blocks

Joseph took a paper napkin and drew a long chain of little circles. He labelled it PROTEIN, then wrote AMINO ACIDS underneath.

Joseph: “Think of a house. Saying you need fifty kilograms of building material tells you very little if you've got bricks but no cement. Protein is like that. Your body uses twenty standard amino acids to build its own proteins. Nine are essential: your body cannot make enough of them, so food must provide them.”
Me: “So the number of grams isn't the whole story?”
Joseph: “Exactly. The amount matters, but so do the building blocks, how well you digest them, and what your body needs them for.”

Joseph tapped the first three names.

Joseph: “Leucine, isoleucine and valine are called branched-chain amino acids, or BCAAs. But don't forget the other six. They're all part of the team.”

He went on to explain that amino acids weren't only about biceps. Tryptophan contributes to serotonin production; phenylalanine is involved in making chemical messengers such as dopamine; and methionine helps with important metabolic processes. The body's chemistry, Joseph said, was a great deal more interesting than a poster of a flexing arm.

John: “So protein is helping my brain as well?”
Me: “Let's not promise miracles, John.”

Leucine: The Fellow Who Rings the Dinner Bell

Joseph picked up his fork and held it like a tiny conductor's baton.

Joseph: “One amino acid is especially interesting: leucine. It helps signal to muscle cells that the materials for repair and rebuilding are arriving. One of the pathways involved is called mTORC1.”
John: “M-T-what? Sounds like a new motorbike.”
Joseph: “Think of leucine as the fellow ringing the factory bell. It can help start muscle protein synthesis. But ringing the bell doesn't manufacture anything unless the other essential amino acids turn up for work.”

Joseph sketched three arrows on the napkin: Protein-rich meal → leucine signal and mTORC1 → muscle protein synthesis, provided the necessary amino acids are available.

He suggested that a meal containing roughly 25–30 g of good-quality protein often supplies around 2–3 g of leucine. That was a useful rule of thumb, not a magic switch. The body's response also depended on exercise, age, the food itself, and the rest of the diet.

Me: “And if I just swallow leucine?”
Joseph: “You might ring the bell, but you still need the builders.”

How Much Protein Is Enough?

John looked at Joseph's plate, then at his own milkshake, as though one of them had betrayed him.

John: “All right. How much protein should a person actually eat?”
Joseph: “Start with body weight and what you're trying to achieve. The usual adult allowance is about 0.8 grams per kilogram each day. But staying strong as you get older, especially if you exercise, can call for more.”

He found the back of a cardboard box and wrote out a few calculations. I kept the idea. Today, with a little computer wizardry that would have astonished our 1980s selves, I've turned Joseph's cardboard arithmetic into a working calculator.

John: “Seventy-five kilos at 1.2? Ninety grams. I can do that.”
Joseph: “Good. Now remember: it's a daily total, not a competition to see who can eat the largest steak in Welkom.”

Steak, Beans, Eggs — and the Great Protein Debate

Joseph pointed at the eggs and chicken on his plate. John pointed hopefully at the milkshake.

Joseph: “Two foods can contain the same amount of protein yet differ in their essential amino acids and digestibility. Animal foods often offer a concentrated, highly digestible mixture. But well-planned plant-based eating can also provide all the essential amino acids.”
Me: “So it's not simply meat good, plants bad?”
Joseph: “Not at all. Soy foods, beans, lentils, nuts and grains can all contribute. Variety and sufficient total protein matter.”

Joseph made another little list. I've expanded it into a modern table using approximate food composition figures. Portions and products vary, so these are guides rather than guarantees.

John: “And my milkshake?”
Joseph: “Milk contains protein. But I wouldn't build your entire nutrition plan around ice cream.”

John looked personally wounded. I ordered another milkshake in solidarity.

The Supplement Shelf: BCAAs, EAAs and the Whole Package

In the gym, brightly labelled tubs promised extraordinary things. Joseph was less impressed by labels than by what was actually inside them.

John: “What about those branched-chain amino acids everybody talks about? Do I need them before training?”
Joseph: “BCAAs give you three essential amino acids. But muscles need all nine. A complete protein food or a properly formulated EAA mixture supplies the full essential set.”

Joseph described how researchers were exploring the effects of different amino acid doses during demanding training and calorie restriction. The idea was that extra EAAs might improve whole-body protein balance, even when an additional improvement in muscle protein synthesis was not detected.

Joseph: “Think of supplements as tools, not miracles. They may help when you can't eat enough, you're short of time, or your appetite is poor. But if you're already eating enough quality protein, a tub of powder may add little.”

A note from the present: Modern studies often examine roughly 8–12 g of EAAs in a serving, but no single dose is best for everyone. Short-term laboratory changes do not automatically mean larger muscles or longer lives.

When the Years Start Catching Up

John leaned back, relieved to discover that protein wasn't only a topic for professional bodybuilders.

Me: “What happens when we're older? Will all this still matter?”
Joseph: “Perhaps even more. As we age, muscle can become less responsive to the same meal or exercise stimulus. Scientists call that anabolic resistance. Combine it with less activity, smaller appetites, illness or too few calories, and keeping muscle becomes harder.”
John: “So the answer is to eat more and sit on the couch?”
Joseph: “Quite the opposite. Train your muscles, eat enough, and give your body the materials to recover. Strength is something worth maintaining, not just showing off.”

He also warned against oversimplifying aging. Not everyone digests protein poorly simply because they've had another birthday. Physical activity, health, diet and individual circumstances all influence the picture.

Outside, the Free State sun had climbed higher. Joseph finished his chicken; John finished his milkshake. Both looked satisfied, though for entirely different reasons.

Me: “So let me get this straight. Protein is the delivery truck, amino acids are the building materials, leucine rings the bell, and training tells the builders where to work?”
Joseph: “Now you're getting it.”
John: “Excellent. Next Saturday can we discuss whether chocolate counts as a vegetable?”

Joseph laughed. I suspected he'd be back.

Final Thoughts — Looking Back from the Present

Decades later, I still remember that Saturday at Snow Haven. The cardboard calculations are gone, the milkshakes are a memory, and my computer can now perform Joseph's arithmetic before John could find a pencil. But the lesson has aged remarkably well.

After revisiting the subject through a modern discussion hosted by Dr Gabrielle Lyon and reviewing the scientific literature, this is what I take from it: protein quantity matters, protein quality matters, and the essential amino acids are central to both. Leucine has a special signaling role, but no single amino acid can replace a balanced diet and the stimulus of exercise.

We now also have stronger evidence that many older adults benefit from paying closer attention to protein intake. What we don't have is convincing proof that taking leucine or EAA supplements on their own will extend human lifespan. And we shouldn't mistake an exciting laboratory signal for a guaranteed improvement in strength, independence or health.

What I'd Tell My Younger Self

If I could return to that table in the Hoefie, I'd tell John to keep his sense of humour — and perhaps ease off the cow jokes. I'd tell myself to focus on a few lasting habits:

  • Eat enough protein over the day. Many healthy older adults can use around 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day as a starting point, with more considered for certain goals or circumstances.
  • Spread it sensibly across meals. Roughly 25–40 g of quality protein at a meal can be practical, without obsessing over a rigid threshold.
  • Include a variety of good protein sources. Animal and well-planned plant-based diets can both meet essential amino acid needs.
  • Keep lifting, moving and staying active. Resistance exercise is one of the most valuable ways to support strength and function.
  • Use supplements only when they solve a real problem. Food first is usually a sensible principle.
  • Judge success by real life. Strength, mobility, independence and wellbeing matter more than a short-lived biochemical reading.

And above all, I'd remember Joseph's simple message: muscle isn't merely something to admire in the mirror. It's something to look after for the years ahead.
