The Future of Strength

A 120-Year-Old's Birthday Wish — The History of Gyms, Final Chapter

Mirandela, the River Tua, and a future that began with an idea. | Bits & Bytes

This is the final instalment of my four-part series, The History of Gyms. If you've missed the earlier chapters, you can find them on my Bits & Bytes blog homepage.

We've travelled from ancient gymnasiums and iron dungeons to fitness empires, social media and wellness ecosystems. Now, for the ending, I'm going to ask you to indulge me.

We're going into the future.

Quite a long way into the future, actually.

Mirandela, Somewhere in Tomorrow

A Golden Sky and a Very Special Cheat Day

I'm sitting in my favourite café overlooking the River Tua in Mirandela. The river still flows beneath the bridge. People still stop to chat. Coffee still smells like coffee.

But the sky is no longer blue.

It's gold.

The River Tua beneath a golden sky — a glimpse of tomorrow.

Generations ago, as the climate crisis deepened, humanity built an enormous orbital sunlight-management system: a network of reflectors and shields designed to regulate how much solar energy reached Earth. Its golden glimmer is now as ordinary to my grandchildren as a blue sky once was to me.

Some people jokingly credit the idea to those old Anunnaki stories about gold and protecting a planet. Zecharia Sitchin would have loved that. The ancient-astronaut claims were never established science, of course, but science fiction has a habit of asking questions that engineers later try to answer.

In this imagined future, the engineers have been rather busy.

Today, though, I'm less interested in planetary engineering than in the enormous slice of birthday cake in front of me.

Because today is my birthday.

My 120th birthday.

And, more importantly, my official cheat day.

Sabrina: "Dad, you've been staring at that cake for five minutes."
Me: "I'm evaluating its nutritional profile."
My wife: "With a fork in your hand?"
Me: "Very advanced equipment, this fork."

My wife, our children and my daughter-in-law are here with me. Their faces have changed over the decades, as mine has. Some people we loved never reached this extraordinary age. We carry their absence quietly, even on the happiest days.

That makes this gathering feel less like an ordinary birthday and more like a gift in itself.

When Muscle Became a Longevity Strategy

Protein, Hypertrophy, and the Science of Staying Strong

At 120, I look something like a muscular man in his seventies. Grey hair? Certainly. A few wrinkles? Naturally. Still capable of a decent workout? You'd better believe it.

In this future, living beyond 150 is no longer unheard of. But the road here began with discoveries that scientists were already investigating back in the 2020s.

Researchers and clinicians, including Dr. Gabrielle Lyon, drew attention to skeletal muscle as much more than a way to fill out a T-shirt. Muscle is active tissue involved in movement, glucose regulation, metabolism and maintaining independence as we age.

And one of the most important processes behind it is muscle protein synthesis: the body's ongoing work of building and repairing muscle proteins.

Resistance training stimulates adaptation. Dietary protein supplies amino acids. Recovery gives the body time to respond. Repeat the process intelligently, and over time you can develop hypertrophy—an increase in muscle size—alongside improvements in strength and function.

It was never as simple as "eat protein, live forever." Biology has never been that obliging. But preserving strength and muscle became one important piece of the healthy-ageing puzzle.

Sabrina: "So you spent a century telling us to eat our protein, and science finally caught up?"
Me: "I prefer the phrase 'patiently ahead of my time'."
My wife: "Patiently?"

There are some debates a man should not enter at 120.

From Guesswork to Precision Nutrition

Precision nutrition, hypertrophy and personalised training — imagined possibilities.

In my youth, we counted calories, weighed food, read nutrition labels and tried to work out how much protein, carbohydrate and fat we needed. Some of us even owned kitchen scales that were more accurate than our bathroom scales.

Now imagine what happens when generations of research meet continuous biological sensing, AI and personalised medicine.

In our fictional future, discreet sensors track metabolic signals, activity, sleep, recovery and changes in body composition. A personalised system estimates nutritional needs and adjusts them as life changes: a harder training day, an illness, a poor night's sleep or a recovery week.

  • Protein targets adapt to training load, age and recovery needs.
  • Carbohydrate intake responds to energy expenditure and activity.
  • Dietary fat, fibre and micronutrient needs are considered together.
  • Training and nutrition plans are updated as the body adapts.
  • Clinicians remain involved when health conditions require professional care.

What once took a coach, a notebook and considerable trial and error can now be estimated with remarkable precision. Not magic. Not perfect certainty. Just far better information, interpreted carefully.

And training has changed too.

Instead of prescribing the same sets and repetitions to everybody, intelligent systems adjust exercise selection, resistance, range of motion, rest and progression to the individual. They watch for fatigue and help balance stimulus with recovery. They distinguish between the goal of building muscle, developing maximal strength and simply remaining capable of carrying your own shopping at 130.

Which, frankly, is a rather underrated fitness goal.

The future of training isn't simply doing more.
It's doing what your body needs—at the right time.

The Birthday Present

From Thirteen Testers to PBH Gyms

My family has been unusually secretive all morning.

Me: "I hope nobody's bought me another fitness watch."
Sabrina: "Dad, you have enough fitness watches to monitor a small country."
My son: "This is something better. Remember PBH?"

Of course I remember PBH.

Progressive Hypertrophy Bodyweight Coach. My app. A little idea that began with a lot of enthusiasm, a fair amount of stubbornness and just thirteen testers.

We put it on something called the Google Play Store. Today, hardly anyone remembers what an Android phone or an app store looked like. But back then, getting an application into people's hands felt like a small miracle.

PBH grew. People trained with it. They shared results. The business expanded, changed hands, and eventually became a worldwide network of immersive training centres.

I watched that happen with a mixture of pride and disbelief. The little coaching app had somehow grown into something much bigger than its creator.

My daughter-in-law: "We've arranged a special PBH session for you."
Me: "Excellent. A nice quiet session?"
Sabrina: "Not exactly."

She hands me the invitation.

It has just three words on it.

WELCOME BACK, EMANUEL.

And beneath them:

Tait's Gym. Welkom. 1982.

Back to Tait's Gym

Some Doors Open into the Past

Later that afternoon, I step into the local PBH gym.

The entrance is sleek and modern. Sensors recognise me. The system confirms my training profile. Somewhere behind the walls, robotic resistance systems and immersive projectors prepare the room.

Then the door opens.

And the future disappears.

Tait's Gym, Welkom — a nostalgic fictional recreation of the 1970s and early 1980s.

The smell of old iron. The heavy punching bag. The worn benches. A couple of bars for chin-ups. Plates that look as if they've been dropped by every strongman in the Free State.

There are no rows of polished machines. No neon slogans telling me to become my best self.

Just the gym I remember.

And then I hear a voice.

Patrick: "Are you planning to train today, Emanuel, or just admire the equipment?"

I turn.

Patrick is there. Chris too. Rui. My brother Jairo. Fernando, the other Fernando, Philip—and familiar faces I haven't seen together in longer than I care to count.

For a second, I forget to breathe.

Some are physically training in their own PBH facilities, connected live from South Africa and other corners of the world. Others are sophisticated AI recreations built from memories, recordings and family archives. They aren't the people we've lost. They can't be. But their familiar gestures and voices make this room feel like a doorway into another time.

I know the difference.

And somehow, it still moves me.

Rui: "Still trying to get those arms bigger?"
Me: "Some projects take longer than others."
Fernando: "At 120, you might finally be ready for the advanced programme."

The room erupts in laughter.

I reach for a barbell. It looks like the old one, but the resistance comes from an

intelligent haptic system.
An intelligent haptic system is an advanced touch-feedback technology that uses sensors, actuators, and artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms to dynamically adapt physical sensations (such as vibrations, forces, or textures) in real time based on user behaviour or digital contexts.
The bar feels heavy. My muscles work. My heart rate rises. Sweat forms on my forehead. The system measures the effort and quietly adjusts the load.

It is real training inside an unreal room.

We lift. We talk. We tease one another about form, old hairstyles and who once claimed to bench more than he actually could.

For a little while, Welkom is no longer thousands of kilometres away.

And forty, sixty, eighty years no longer seem like such impossible distances.

It's not simply virtual reality.

It's a reunion across continents, generations and, in a fictional sense, even death.

At the end of the session, I sit on an old bench. The others are still joking. Somewhere, one of those voices belongs to someone I can never meet again outside this room.

My eyes sting a little.

I'll blame the sweat.

How the PBH Gym Works

When AI, Immersion and Exercise Science Meet

The experience feels impossible to someone from 2026. But each part has roots in technologies already being explored: virtual and mixed reality, adaptive resistance machines, motion tracking, wearable sensors, exercise apps and AI-assisted coaching.

In this imagined future, PBH brings them together:

  • Immersive worlds: recreate a 1980s gym, an ancient Greek training ground or a mountain landscape.
  • Adaptive resistance: make virtual weights produce measurable muscular effort through physical resistance systems.
  • Shared training: connect friends in distant cities as if they were in the same room.
  • AI coaching: adjust technique feedback, exercise intensity, progressive overload and recovery.
  • Digital companions: offer clearly identified simulations of people from the past, with appropriate consent and safeguards.
  • Precision nutrition: link training demands to individual nutrition and health guidance.

The equipment is extraordinary. But the cleverest thing PBH has done isn't to make the weights feel real.

It's to make being together feel possible again.

A Longer Life Is Still a Choice

The Great Fitness Paradox, One Century Later

Back outside, I see people strolling beside the Tua. Some are running. Others are sitting and watching the river.

Not everyone in this future is fit. Not everyone lives to 150. Even with remarkable medical advances, people face illness, unequal access to care, personal limitations and choices that affect health.

Some still fall for miracle products and conspiracy theories. Some hope that a pill or treatment will do everything for them. Others simply struggle to find the time, resources or support to be active.

Medicine remains essential. Exercise doesn't replace it. And health is never just a matter of willpower.

But one old truth has survived every technological revolution:

Knowing what helps us live well
is not the same as doing it.

Technology can guide us. It can remove barriers. It can make training safer, more personal and more enjoyable.

It still cannot decide to show up on our behalf.

Thirteen

Where a Small Beginning Can Lead

Later that evening, the café is quieter. The golden light has softened over the river, and my family is still talking about the birthday surprise.

But my thoughts have wandered back to PBH.

To the beginning.

To an idea, a little application, and thirteen people who agreed to test it.

Thirteen.

The number brings another image to mind.

More than two thousand years ago, according to the Gospel accounts, Jesus shared a final meal with his twelve apostles. Thirteen around a table. Ordinary men, in simple clothes, from an ordinary-looking corner of the world.

Many people of their time would have overlooked them.

Yet the message of love associated with Jesus and carried forward by his followers went on to shape countless lives across centuries.

“You shall love your neighbour as yourself.”

Jesus Christ — Matthew 22:39

I'm not comparing a fitness app to that spiritual and historical legacy. Not remotely.

But the number makes me think about beginnings—and how we rarely know where a small act of faith, generosity or encouragement might lead.

Thirteen people believed enough in my idea to give it a try.

In this imagined future, those thirteen testers helped set something remarkable in motion.

Not because they changed the world overnight.

But because they started.

Final Thoughts

What the History of Gyms Was Really About

When I began this series, we travelled back to the Greeks and their gymnasiums, places where physical development, learning and community belonged together.

We passed through strongmen, bodybuilding legends, iron dungeons, commercial fitness clubs, boutique studios and digital communities.

And now we've arrived at a gym that can recreate almost any place in human history.

Yet after all that progress, what did I choose for my 120th birthday?

A simple old gym.

A few benches.

Some iron.

And my friends.

Perhaps the history of gyms was never really the history of buildings, machines or even muscles.

Perhaps it was always the history of people trying to become stronger—and finding one another along the way.

Different centuries.
Different equipment.
The same human heart.

Back to 2026 — One Last Invitation

And now, back to the present. No golden sky. No 120th birthday. No holographic reunion waiting at Tait's Gym.

Just me, working on PBH, hoping to build something genuinely useful—one improvement and one tester at a time.

That future is only a story today.

But every real invention begins somewhere.
