From Iron Dungeons to Lifestyle Empires

The History of Gyms – Part IV

By João Rolo

The morning sun had only just begun painting golden reflections across the River Tua.

Mirandela was waking up slowly.

Dog walkers moved along the riverside paths. Cyclists drifted past. Joggers followed familiar routes. A few early risers glided silently across the pedestrian bridge on electric scooters.

The town always felt like it was permanently on holiday.

Which, honestly, was one of the reasons Sabrina loved living there.

Beside her walked her father, Emanuel.

Officially in his early sixties.

Unofficially powered by some mysterious force that allowed him to have the energy levels of a man twenty years younger.

He carried two shaker bottles.

Sabrina already knew exactly what was inside.

Protein. Carbohydrates. Electrolytes.

Carefully measured.

Because her father never did anything halfway.

Sabrina: "You know most people just drink coffee before a workout?"
Emanuel: "Most people also complain about having no energy."

Sabrina laughed.

That was classic Dad.

A lifetime spent in gyms, sports fields, running tracks, swimming pools, nutrition books, and fitness magazines had given him an answer for everything.

Sometimes whether you asked for it or not.

They crossed the bridge together.

Ahead of them stood the local gym.

Today was special.

Her father was joining her workout.

Which meant she would probably discover muscles she didn't know existed.

As they stepped through the gym doors, Emanuel slowed his pace slightly.

His eyes scanned the room with the curiosity of an explorer arriving in a foreign land.

Machines everywhere.

Rows of them.

Machines with cables.

Machines with screens.

Machines that looked as though they belonged on a spacecraft rather than inside a gym.

Some he recognised immediately.

Many he had never seen before.

He walked slowly between the rows.

Looking.

Searching.

Then he frowned slightly.

Emanuel: "Where are the barbells?"

Sabrina laughed.

Sabrina: "They're over there."

She pointed toward a small corner of the gym.

A modest rack of dumbbells sat against the wall.

Nearby stood a single squat rack.

One.

Surrounded by what felt like three hundred machines.

Emanuel looked around again.

Then shook his head and smiled.

Emanuel: "Interesting."

That single word usually meant a lecture was loading in the background.

I knew the signs.

Emanuel: "Let's start with the Clean and Press."
Sabrina: "The what?"

He laughed.

Emanuel: "The Clean and Press."
Emanuel: "One of the best exercises ever invented."

That got her attention.

Sabrina: "Better than all these machines?"
Emanuel: "Most of them."

He pointed toward the barbell.

Emanuel: "If I could only choose a handful of exercises for the rest of my life..."
Emanuel: "I'd choose Clean and Presses, Squats, Deadlifts, Chin-ups and Bench Presses."
Sabrina: ""Why those?"
Emanuel: "Because they're compound exercises."
Sabrina: "And what exactly is a compound exercise?"

Emanuel adjusted the weights while he answered.

Emanuel: "People hear the word 'compound' and think it sounds complicated."
Emanuel: "But it's actually the opposite."
Emanuel: "A compound exercise simply trains the body the way it was designed to move."

He picked the barbell up from the floor.

Explosively.

Smoothly.

Then pressed it overhead.

Emanuel: "Look at this movement."
Emanuel: "I'm lifting something from the ground and placing it overhead."
Emanuel: "Humans have been doing variations of this for thousands of years."

He lowered the barbell again.

Emanuel: "Farmers loading sacks of grain."
Emanuel: "Builders moving materials."
Emanuel: "Parents lifting children."
Emanuel: "Real-world movement."

He gestured toward the machine section.

Emanuel: "The problem is that modern life doesn't require much of that anymore."
Emanuel: "We sit."
Emanuel: "We drive."
Emanuel: "We press buttons."
Emanuel: "Then we wonder why our backs hurt."

I couldn't argue with that.

Sabrina: "So you're saying one exercise can do more than several machines?"
Emanuel: "Absolutely."
Emanuel: "If someone came to the gym and did three good sets of Clean and Presses..."
Emanuel: "...they'd probably gain more useful strength than spending an hour doing bicep curls and tricep extensions."

Several nearby gym-goers suddenly looked personally attacked.

Emanuel either didn't notice or pretended not to.

Emanuel: "Isolation exercises have their place."
Emanuel: "But they should never be the foundation."
Emanuel: "Build the house first."
Emanuel: "Then paint the walls."

That analogy even made sense to Sabrina.

Sabrina: "So what should be the foundation?"
Emanuel: "Simple."
Emanuel: "Spend eighty to ninety percent of your training time on compound movements."
Emanuel: "Squat."
Emanuel: "Push."
Emanuel: "Pull."
Emanuel: "Carry."
Emanuel: "Lift."
Emanuel: "Move."

For a moment, I found myself thinking back to everything we'd already discussed throughout this series.

Ancient hunters.

Greek athletes.

Strongmen.

Farmers.

Builders.

Different centuries.

Different tools.

The same movements.

Maybe the history of gyms wasn't really the history of equipment after all.

Maybe it was the history of humans trying to remember how they were meant to move.

Two hours later...

Sabrina was sitting in a restaurant overlooking the river.

Every muscle felt alive.

Not sore.

Alive.

The kind of feeling that only comes from a genuinely good workout.

Her arms tingled.

Her legs felt pleasantly heavy.

Her posture somehow felt taller.

Even breathing felt easier.

She looked across the table at her father.

He seemed completely unaffected.

Which felt deeply unfair.

Sabrina: "I don't understand how you're not tired."
Emanuel: "I am tired."
Sabrina: "You don't look tired."
Emanuel: "That's because I've had sixty years to practice."

Their food arrived.

The owner smiled.

He already knew their order.

Extra-large grilled steak.

Two eggs.

Rice.

Air-fried chips.

The perfect compromise between enjoyment and responsibility.

Sabrina took a bite.

The conversation drifted naturally back toward the topic they had been discussing all week.

Gyms.

Fitness.

History.

Sabrina: "Something still puzzles me."
Emanuel: "Only one thing?"
Sabrina: "Very funny."
Sabrina: "Last time we spoke about how gyms evolved through history."
Sabrina: "But at some point they became... bigger than gyms."
Sabrina: "They became brands."
Sabrina: "How did that happen?"

Emanuel smiled.

He put down his fork.

Then pointed toward the gym they had trained at earlier.

Emanuel: "Because eventually people discovered something important."
Emanuel: "Gyms don't really sell exercise."
Sabrina: "What do they sell then?"
Emanuel: "Hope."

That answer stayed with me.

Because the deeper we dig into fitness history, the more obvious something becomes:

The story stopped being about equipment a long time ago.

It became a story about people.

From Hardcore Gyms to Mainstream Fitness

The End of the Warehouse Era

The gyms of the 1960s, 1970s and early 1980s were very different from what most people imagine today.

There were no smoothie bars.

No smartphone apps.

No ambient lighting.

No recovery lounges.

No motivational wall art reminding you to "live your best life."

There was iron.

Lots of iron.

Concrete floors.

Rusted weights.

Chalk.

Sweat.

Determination.

And occasionally someone screaming through a deadlift attempt that looked capable of summoning thunderstorms.

From Hardcore Training Halls to Modern Fitness Centres

Many of these places felt more like workshops than businesses.

They existed primarily for one purpose:

Getting stronger.

Places like Gold's Gym and Muscle Beach became legendary.

Particularly in Venice Beach, California.

Bodybuilders trained outdoors.

Powerlifters pushed enormous weights.

Strongmen displayed extraordinary feats of strength.

Fitness wasn't mainstream.

It was a subculture.

A tribe.

And tribes often appear intimidating to outsiders.

Sabrina: "So if I walked into one of those gyms back then..."
Emanuel: "You'd probably spend five minutes wondering if you'd entered a gym or a prison yard."

I nearly choked on my drink.

Emanuel: "I'm only half joking."

The truth is that many ordinary people felt uncomfortable in those environments.

The average office worker.

The average parent.

The average retiree.

They weren't the target audience.

The old gym wasn't designed for everyone.

It was designed for people obsessed with improvement.

People who loved training itself.

But eventually somebody noticed something.

A very important something.

The real market wasn't bodybuilders.

The real market was everybody else.

The Fitness Boom of the 1990s

Selling Memberships Instead of Muscles

By the late 1980s and early 1990s, fitness companies began thinking very differently.

Instead of asking:

"How do we attract serious athletes?"

They started asking:

"How do we attract everyone?"

That question changed the industry forever.

Large commercial chains expanded rapidly.

Companies such as:

  • Fitness First
  • Virgin Active
  • Planet Fitness
  • Numerous regional fitness chains

began targeting ordinary people rather than elite athletes.

Suddenly the gym wasn't being marketed as a place for bodybuilders.

It became a place for:

  • Office workers
  • Parents
  • Students
  • Retirees
  • Beginners

The business model changed dramatically.

Gyms were no longer selling muscles.

They were selling:

  • Convenience
  • Cleanliness
  • Accessibility
  • Safety
  • Comfort

Memberships exploded worldwide.

Millions of people who had never considered joining a gym suddenly found themselves walking through the doors.

Fitness had become mainstream.

The Fitness Boom of the 1990s

Sabrina: "So gyms figured out most people didn't want to become Arnold Schwarzenegger?"

Emanuel laughed.

Emanuel: "That's actually funnier than you think."
Emanuel: "Years ago, someone told Arnold they didn't want to look like him."
Sabrina: "That sounds risky."
Emanuel: "Arnold's reply became legendary."
Emanuel: "\"Don't worry, you never will.\""

Sabrina burst out laughing.

Sabrina: "Okay, that's actually brilliant."
Emanuel: "And it perfectly illustrates the point."
Emanuel: "Most people never walked into a gym dreaming of becoming world champion bodybuilders."
Emanuel: "Most people simply wanted to feel healthier, have more energy, lose a few kilos, feel more confident, or yes..."
Emanuel: "...fit comfortably into their jeans again."

That simple observation unlocked billions of dollars.

Because once fitness became accessible, it stopped being a niche hobby.

It became part of everyday life.

But something even more interesting was about to happen.

Gyms weren't just becoming more popular.

They were becoming better at marketing.

Much better.

And that changed everything.

The Birth of Fitness Marketing

Selling the Dream

The waiter refilled our drinks.

Sabrina stared out across the River Tua for a moment.

A group of cyclists rolled past the riverside promenade.

Two joggers crossed the pedestrian bridge we had walked across earlier that morning.

Everywhere she looked, people were moving.

Which raised another question.

Sabrina: "If gyms became more accessible in the 1990s, what happened next?"
Emanuel: "Marketing happened."
Sabrina: "That sounded slightly sinister."
Emanuel: "Only because it worked so well."

He wasn't wrong.

During the late twentieth century, something fascinating occurred.

Gyms gradually realised that people weren't buying exercise.

They were buying outcomes.

More specifically...

They were buying feelings.

And feelings are far easier to market than treadmills.

Early fitness advertising focused almost entirely on strength.

Lift heavier.

Run faster.

Become stronger.

Simple.

Direct.

Functional.

But then marketers noticed something important.

Most people weren't motivated by strength itself.

They were motivated by what strength represented.

Confidence.

Attractiveness.

Success.

Energy.

Self-esteem.

Social acceptance.

Suddenly the advertising changed.

Dramatically.

  • Strength became confidence.
  • Fitness became attractiveness.
  • Exercise became transformation.
  • Health became lifestyle.
  • Membership became belonging.

Fitness magazines exploded in popularity.

Glossy covers featured extraordinary physiques.

Headlines promised life-changing transformations.

Advertisements suggested that a better body could unlock a better life.

Television soon joined the party.

Commercials showed smiling people jogging along beaches.

Attractive couples working out together.

Happy families riding bicycles.

Nobody was shown struggling through their sixth set of squats.

Nobody was shown cleaning sweat off gym benches.

Marketing rarely sells reality.

Marketing sells aspiration.

The Fitness Marketing Machine

Then came perhaps the most powerful tool of all.

The transformation story.

Before-and-after photographs appeared everywhere.

They appeared in magazines.

Television advertisements.

Posters.

Websites.

Social media.

They all told the same story:

You are not stuck.

You can change.

That's a message far more powerful than any piece of equipment.

Emanuel smiled.

Emanuel: "Nobody buys a treadmill because they love treadmills."
Sabrina: "That makes sense."
Emanuel: "People buy what they hope life will feel like afterward."

That single sentence may explain modern fitness marketing better than an entire university textbook.

Because nobody truly wants a treadmill.

They want:

  • More energy.
  • Better health.
  • Greater confidence.
  • Reduced stress.
  • A longer life.
  • A second chance.

The equipment simply became the vehicle.

The dream was always the destination.

Supplements, Shakes and the Billion-Dollar Machine

Fitness Leaves the Gym

At this point our food had mostly disappeared.

Which, considering the size of the steaks, felt like an achievement worthy of its own fitness award.

Sabrina took a sip of water.

Sabrina: "So gyms became businesses."
Emanuel: "Much bigger than that."
Emanuel: "Fitness escaped the gym."

That might sound strange.

But it's exactly what happened.

Once fitness became mainstream, entire industries emerged around it.

Industries worth billions.

First came supplements.

Protein powders existed before, but demand exploded.

Your local grocer is selling protein now, next to the veggies, chocolates and fresh bread.

Suddenly store shelves filled with products promising:

  • More muscle.
  • Faster recovery.
  • Greater endurance.
  • Improved performance.
  • Enhanced fat loss.

Whey protein became a household name.

Creatine became one of the most researched sports supplements in history.

Pre-workouts promised explosive energy.

Recovery drinks promised faster results.

Meal replacements promised convenience.

Fat burners promised miracles.

Some delivered.

Most delivered mostly marketing.

Emanuel raised an eyebrow.

Emanuel: "What do I always say about products you buy?"
Sabrina: "Always read the read the Nutrition Facts label and the ingredient list. "
Emanuel: "Yes".
"If there are more that five ingredients or words you can't pronounce, don't buy it. If you can't read it, don't know what it is , you should not eat it. " "You should, at the bare minimum, know what you are eating"
Emanuel: "Fitness companies discovered something magical."
Sabrina: "What's that?"
Emanuel: "People don't stop being customers when they leave the gym."

And he was absolutely right.

Soon fitness extended into clothing.

Global brands transformed athletic apparel into everyday fashion.

Companies like:

  • Nike
  • Under Armour
  • Gymshark
  • Lululemon

helped turn fitness into something people could wear.

Exercise clothing no longer lived exclusively inside gyms.

It appeared in shopping centres.

Airports.

Coffee shops.

Universities.

Offices.

Entire wardrobes began revolving around active lifestyles.

Then came accessories.

Water bottles.

Shaker cups.

Fitness watches.

Recovery tools.

Smart scales.

Resistance bands.

Fitness was no longer something you did.

It became something you carried.

Something you wore.

Something you displayed.

Fitness Leaves the Gym

Sabrina: "Wait..."
Sabrina: "So even my water bottle is part of fitness culture?"
Emanuel: "Congratulations."
Emanuel: "You're a walking revenue stream."

Sabrina laughed so hard nearby diners glanced over.

But beneath the joke was an important truth.

Fitness had become far more than exercise.

It had become an ecosystem.

A network of products, services, brands, communities, technologies and experiences all built around one idea:

Helping people become a better version of themselves.

Or at least helping them believe they could.

The Rise of Boutique Fitness

People Wanted Community Again

The afternoon sun was beginning to drift lower over Mirandela.

The River Tua shimmered beneath the bridge.

Around us, people continued their weekend routines.

Walking.

Running.

Cycling.

Living.

Sabrina leaned back in her chair.

Sabrina: "So by this point fitness had become a business."
Emanuel: "A very big business."
Sabrina: "Then why did people start leaving traditional gyms for things like CrossFit, yoga studios, and group training classes?"

Emanuel smiled.

He clearly enjoyed this question.

Emanuel: "Because people eventually remembered something ancient."
Sabrina: "What's that?"
Emanuel: "Human beings rarely do difficult things alone."

That simple observation explains one of the most important developments in modern fitness.

During the early 2000s, many gyms were successful.

But they often felt impersonal.

Huge rooms.

Endless rows of machines.

Hundreds of members.

Yet strangely little connection.

People entered.

Trained.

Left.

Sometimes without speaking to another human being.

Then a new idea emerged.

What if fitness wasn't only about exercise?

What if it was about belonging?

Suddenly boutique fitness exploded.

Smaller.

More personal.

More community-driven.

More tribal.

And people loved it.

Perhaps no example illustrates this better than CrossFit.

On the surface it looked like a training methodology.

Functional movements.

High intensity.

Constant variation.

But beneath the workouts was something much more powerful.

Community.

Shared suffering.

Shared victories.

Shared identity.

People didn't merely attend CrossFit gyms.

They joined tribes.

The same pattern appeared elsewhere.

    F45 Training is a global fitness community specializing in high-intensity interval training (HIIT), circuit training, and functional training packed into 45 minutes.   • OrangeTheory (click = more..)

    Orangetheory Fitness (OTF) is a science-backed, tech-driven group workout that combines heart rate-based interval training with functional strength training inside a 60-minute session.   • SoulCycle (click = more..)

    SoulCycle is an immersive, high-energy indoor cycling workout that blends intense cardiovascular interval training with choreography and mental mindfulness. Founded in 2006 in New York City, the brand transformed traditional spinning into a dark, candlelit group fitness experience focused on community, rhythm, and self-care.

  • Yoga Studios (click = more..)

    Yoga studios are dedicated spaces designed for the practice of yoga, meditation, and breathwork. Unlike mainstream gyms, yoga studios focus heavily on creating a calm environment—often featuring soft lighting, minimalist decor, aromatherapy, and a quiet atmosphere to promote mental clarity and physical well-being.

  • Pilates Studios (click = more..)

    Pilates studios are specialized fitness facilities dedicated to the training method developed by Joseph Pilates in the early 20th century. This practice focuses on low-impact flexibility, muscular endurance, and core strength, emphasizing precise alignment, breath control, and a strong mind-body connection.

  • Small Group Training Facilities (click = more..)

    Small Group Training (SGT) facilities are boutique fitness spaces that bridge the gap between large, crowded gym classes and expensive 1-on-1 personal training. Typically capping attendance at 4 to 10 participants per session, these facilities allow a single coach to provide personalized form correction, customized weight selections, and individual accountability within a shared, motivating team environment.

Each offered something traditional gyms often struggled to provide.

Accountability.

Encouragement.

Connection.

A reason to keep showing up.

The Rise of Boutique Fitness

Sabrina: "So people weren't just buying workouts."
Emanuel: "Exactly."
Emanuel: "They were buying accountability."
Emanuel: "Friendship."
Emanuel: "Identity."
Emanuel: "The same reason humans have joined communities for thousands of years."

That observation struck me.

Because throughout history, from Greek gymnasiums to modern fitness studios, the equipment changed repeatedly.

The need to belong never did.

Humans join communities.

Fitness simply became the latest version.

The Instagram Revolution

When Every Workout Became Content

If boutique fitness changed how people trained...

Social media changed how people saw fitness.

Forever.

Emanuel looked around the restaurant.

Three different people were taking photos of their meals.

One was recording a video.

Another was carefully repositioning a coffee cup for better lighting.

He pointed discreetly.

Emanuel: "Twenty years ago nobody would have believed that one day people would photograph their lunch before eating it."
Sabrina: "To be fair..."

Yet the same phenomenon happened inside gyms.

Platforms such as:

  • Instagram
  • YouTube
  • Facebook
  • TikTok

transformed fitness completely.

For the first time in history:

Everyone became a broadcaster.

Everyone became a publisher.

Everyone became a brand.

Fitness advice no longer came exclusively from magazines, books, coaches, or television personalities.

It came from everywhere.

And everyone.

A teenager with a smartphone could reach more people than an entire fitness magazine from the 1980s.

That changed the rules.

Overnight.

Influencers emerged.

Fitness creators built audiences numbering in the millions.

Transformation stories spread globally.

Training tips travelled instantly.

Knowledge became more accessible than ever before.

Which was wonderful.

And occasionally problematic.

Because social media also introduced comparison culture.

People stopped comparing themselves only to neighbours.

They started comparing themselves to the entire world.

Often filtered.

Edited.

Enhanced.

Curated.

The strongest competition in any gym is rarely the person standing next to you.

It's the person you were yesterday.

Not suddenly.

Gradually.

Almost quietly.

One day a gym was a room full of equipment.

The next it had:

  • A recovery area
  • A sauna
  • An ice bath

  • A nutrition coach

Some even added coworking spaces.

Meditation rooms.

Massage services.

Recovery lounges.

Entire ecosystems developed around health.

Fitness was no longer simply exercise.

It became wellness.

Physical health.

Mental health.

Recovery.

Nutrition.

Community.

Lifestyle.

The Modern Wellness Ecosystem

Different equipment.

Different buildings.

Different technology.

Health.

Purpose.

Growth.

Connection.

The more fitness evolves...

The more it seems to rediscover old truths.

By the time the second round of coffees arrived, the conversation had moved far beyond dumbbells.

Eat lunch.

Meet friends.

Attend yoga classes.

Recover in a sauna.

Then perhaps squeeze in a workout before heading home.

History has a habit of travelling in circles.

The Great Fitness Paradox

More Fitness Than Ever. More Inactivity Than Ever.

The afternoon sun reflected off the river as Emanuel became unusually quiet.

Whenever that happened, it usually meant he was about to say something worth remembering.

Emanuel: "Can I tell you something ironic?"
Sabrina: "Always."
Emanuel: "We live in the most fitness-aware period in human history."

And he was right.

Today we have:

  • More gyms than ever before
  • More exercise equipment
  • More nutrition knowledge
  • More fitness content
  • More training programs
  • More wearable technology

Yet globally we also see:

  • Rising obesity rates
  • Increasing screen time
  • More sedentary lifestyles
  • Less daily movement
Sabrina: "How is that even possible?"
Emanuel: "Because knowledge isn't the same as action."

That sentence landed harder than expected.

Emanuel: "Technology solved many problems."
Emanuel: "But it also removed movement from daily life."
Emanuel: "Our ancestors walked because they had to."
Emanuel: "We drive because we can."
Emanuel: "They climbed stairs."
Emanuel: "We search for elevators."
Emanuel: "They moved by necessity."
Emanuel: "We schedule movement into calendars."

The strange truth is that modern fitness exists largely because modern life became so inactive.

We built gyms because everyday life stopped being one.

What This Means for Us

The conversation slowed.

The plates were nearly empty.

The river drifted quietly beyond the restaurant windows.

Emanuel folded his hands and looked thoughtful.

Emanuel: "People think gyms sell fitness."

He paused.

Emanuel: "Mostly they sell hope."

I thought about that for a moment.

Hope for:

  • Better health
  • Greater confidence
  • More energy
  • A fresh start
  • Belonging
  • Transformation

And suddenly the entire history of gyms looked different.

Ancient warriors trained for survival.

Greek philosophers trained for excellence.

Strongmen trained for spectacle.

Bodybuilders trained for aesthetics.

Modern gym members train for hundreds of different reasons.

Yet underneath all those centuries lies the same human desire.

The desire to become something more than we were yesterday.

Different centuries.

Different equipment.

Same hope.

For a moment, the conversation paused.

Sabrina was watching the river.

People were still walking along the promenade.

Some were jogging.

Others were cycling.

A few walked their dogs.

Life carried on around us.

Emanuel leaned back in his chair and smiled quietly.

As the conversation drifted for a moment, his thoughts wandered elsewhere.

He thought about Sabrina.

About the young woman sitting across from him.

Despite the challenges life had placed in her path, she kept moving forward.

She kept learning.

She kept growing.

And now she was building strength in the gym as well.

He felt an enormous sense of pride.

Then his thoughts turned to his son.

To his daughter-in-law.

Roxy, their cousin

All three living far away.

All three were missed more than they probably realised.

A deep sorrow suddenly hits him hard, BB "How I miss you"

Yet every time he heard what they were doing, they seemed to be moving too.

Playing pickleball.

Staying active.

Embracing life with the same enthusiasm they always had.

Then he thought about his wife.

The woman who rarely missed an opportunity to get in her daily steps.

Whether it was a walk through town, a stroll in the local park (Parque Verde), or simply finding reasons to stay active throughout the day.

Movement had become part of her life.

Just as it had become part of theirs.

A warm feeling settled over him.

Not pride in himself.

Pride in them.

Because fitness was never really about six-packs.

Or personal records.

Or gym memberships.

It was about having the strength and health to enjoy life.

To play.

To explore.

To keep showing up for the people you love.

And as he watched the river flowing past the restaurant, he found himself wondering something.

Had he influenced any of this?

Had all those years spent training, learning, talking about nutrition, encouraging movement and sharing his passion for health left a small mark on the people closest to him?

He honestly didn't know.

Perhaps parents never really do.

But looking around at the people he loved most, all choosing active and healthy lives in their own way, he couldn't help but smile.

Maybe.

Just maybe.

Where We Go Next

The shadows were growing longer across the river when we finally stood up to leave.

As always, one conversation had somehow become a journey through centuries.

But the story is far from over.

Because the next chapter may be the most fascinating one yet.

The future.

A future where questions are already emerging:

  • Will AI replace personal trainers?
  • Will virtual gyms replace physical gyms?
  • Will exercise become personalised medicine?
  • Will wearable technology become essential healthcare tools?
  • Will gyms become more digital—or more human?
Sabrina: "So what do you think the gym of the future looks like?"

Emanuel smiled.

Emanuel: "The future gym might know your heartbeat before you do."
Emanuel: "But it'll still be trying to solve the same problem humans have been solving for thousands of years."
Sabrina: "Which is?"
Emanuel: "How to become stronger."

He stood up and picked up his jacket.

Emanuel: "Not just physically."

And perhaps that has always been the real history of gyms.

Not the history of buildings.

The history of people trying to improve themselves.
