Before the Machines: How Exercise Was Born

The History of Gyms – Part III

By João Rolo

Joseph leaned back in his chair at Snow Haven, sunlight sliding across the table like spilled honey. Chris was scrolling through fitness videos on his phone.
Reader - "Hey, what, but we did not have smart phones those day." 😵‍💫
Me - "Have you met Chris' grandpa grandpa, Doc Brown."
"If not , when you finished reading this, click here and you'll understand how we had smart phones in the 80s."😂🤣

Chris: "Look at this one. ‘Seven-minute anti-aging workout used by billionaires.’ Apparently all I need is this app and a cold plunge."

Joseph laughed into his coffee.

Joseph: "Human beings have been trying to sell immortality since the pyramids."

I looked at him.

Me: "So what did people do before gyms?"

Joseph smiled.

Joseph: "That’s the wrong question."

He pointed out the window.

Joseph: "The real question is: when did humans stop moving naturally… and start needing special buildings just to remember how?"

That line stayed with me.

Because somewhere between hunting fields, battle grounds, factory floors, suburban offices, and glowing smartphone screens… movement changed.

Not physically.

Philosophically.

And if the first two parts of this series explored why movement shapes the mind and how gym culture evolved socially, then this chapter goes deeper still.

Far deeper.

Back to the very beginning.

Long Before Protein Shakes

Long before stainless steel dumbbells… long before treadmills… long before somebody decided a shaker bottle needed three separate compartments… human beings were already training.

Not for beach bodies.

For survival.

Ancient hunters sprinted across uneven terrain, climbed rocks, carried carcasses, threw spears, dragged supplies, and walked distances modern office workers would call "a hiking challenge."

Movement wasn’t scheduled. It was life itself.

Joseph once told us something interesting while stirring sugar into his coffee.

Joseph: "The human body wasn’t designed for comfort. It was designed for adaptation."

And ancient civilizations understood this instinctively.

In Ancient Egypt, murals show wrestling, stick fighting, swimming, rowing, and strength contests. Soldiers trained for endurance because entire empires depended on physical readiness.

In Ancient China, movement became connected not only to war but also to health and balance. Exercises resembling early martial arts and breathing systems emerged centuries before modern wellness culture rediscovered “mind-body connection.”

Chris nearly spat out his milkshake when Joseph explained that ancient people had already figured out mobility training.

Chris: "So you’re telling me influencers didn’t invent stretching?"
Joseph: "No. They just added ring lights."

But nowhere did physical culture flourish more visibly than in Ancient Greece.

The Greek gymnasion was more than a training hall. It was part university. Part athletic academy. Part philosophical playground.

Young men trained in running, wrestling, discus, spear throwing, and combat sports while also debating mathematics, politics, ethics, and poetry.

To the Greeks, a powerful mind and a disciplined body belonged together. Separating them would have seemed absurd.

At Plato’s Academy and Aristotle’s Lyceum, physical training lived beside intellectual inquiry. Sweat and philosophy shared the same courtyard.

Imagine that for a second.

One man discussing virtue under olive trees while another practices wrestling nearby. No Bluetooth headphones. No motivational EDM playlist. No "Summer Body Challenge."

Just movement. Purpose. Discipline. Community.

The Greeks believed physical weakness affected moral weakness. Strength was not vanity. It was responsibility.

And honestly? That idea feels strangely modern again.

Because after centuries of treating fitness mostly as aesthetics, people are once again rediscovering something ancient:

From Egyptian Warriors to Chinese Masters

Movement changes the mind as much as the body.

The Quiet Centuries

History, however, rarely moves in straight lines.

The fall of the Roman Empire changed nearly everything in Europe. Trade fractured. Cities shrank. War, famine, disease, and survival took center stage.

Structured physical culture faded.

Not because people stopped moving. Far from it.

Farmers worked fields from sunrise to sunset. Blacksmiths hammered iron with shoulders that modern gyms would envy. Builders hauled stone without "functional training" seminars.

But movement became necessity again — not philosophy.

There were no carefully designed fitness systems. No public gymnasiums. No scientific exercise methods.

And perhaps most importantly… there was little leisure.

Joseph explained it beautifully.

Joseph: "People don’t invent jogging clubs when they’re trying not to starve."

The Middle Ages valued strength differently.

Knights trained for combat. Archers developed extraordinary upper-body power. Soldiers marched impossible distances.

But these were military requirements. Not health practices.

Meanwhile, ordinary citizens remained physically active simply because daily life demanded it. Walking miles was normal. Manual labor was unavoidable.

Ironically, the less technologically advanced society was, the less people needed "exercise" as a separate activity.

That silence lasted centuries.

Then came industrialization.

And suddenly human beings began sitting.

A lot.

The Quite Centuries

"If you stop movement, then this is the first step to death," - Arnold Schwarzenegger

The 1800s: When Exercise Became Scientific

The Industrial Revolution transformed civilization.

Factories replaced farms. Machines replaced labor. Cities expanded. People spent longer hours indoors.

For the first time in history, large populations moved less while working more.

Sound familiar?

Doctors, educators, and military leaders began noticing something worrying: modern life was making people weaker.

That concern sparked one of the biggest turning points in fitness history.

Exercise stopped being accidental.

It became intentional.

In Sweden, Pehr Henrik Ling developed systems of controlled movement based on anatomy and posture. His methods emphasized balance, breathing, flexibility, and rehabilitation.

Today we’d probably market it as "corrective mobility training."

Joseph called it "physiotherapy before physiotherapists existed."

Meanwhile in Germany, Friedrich Ludwig Jahn promoted outdoor physical training using climbing frames, bars, rings, and structured drills. His movement spread rapidly through Europe.

For the first time, exercise systems were being taught methodically.

Schools adopted them. Military organizations adopted them. Communities adopted them.

Then things became commercial.

In Paris, Hippolyte Triat opened one of the first major public gymnasiums in the mid-1800s. People paid membership fees to improve themselves physically.

Which means, technically speaking, gym subscriptions are older than electricity in many cities.

Chris: "Imagine paying gym fees before air conditioning existed."
Joseph: "That’s because those people were tougher than us."

Triat’s gym included ropes, bars, climbing equipment, free weights, and resistance systems that would look surprisingly familiar today.

And then came the strongmen.

Not movie superheroes. Real ones.

Traveling performers demonstrated impossible feats of strength before television existed. They bent iron bars, lifted horses, balanced enormous weights, and challenged crowds publicly.

Eugen Sandow became perhaps the most famous of them all.

Unlike earlier strongmen who focused purely on spectacle, Sandow promoted aesthetics.
Symmetry.
Proportion.
Muscular beauty.

He transformed strength into aspiration.

Suddenly exercise wasn’t only about war, labor, or survival.

It became appearance.

Identity.

Self-improvement.

In many ways, modern fitness culture begins there.

The 1800s marked the birth of scientific exercise and the modern gym

"Health is a divine gift, and the care of the body is a sacred duty, to neglect which is to sin." - Eugen Sandow

The 20th Century: Muscles Meet Media

If the 1800s professionalized exercise… then the 1900s commercialized it.

Massively.

Magazines exploded in popularity. Posters of muscular physiques appeared everywhere. Exercise manuals promised confidence, attractiveness, masculinity, vitality, and success.

Fitness became marketing.

And marketing became powerful.

By the mid-20th century, television carried exercise directly into people’s homes.

Figures like Jack LaLanne encouraged ordinary families to move their bodies daily. He spoke about nutrition, consistency, and long-term health decades before wellness became trendy.

Chris looked genuinely shocked when Joseph told him LaLanne exercised into his nineties.

Chris: "Meanwhile my knees sound like microwave popcorn when I stand up."

Then bodybuilding exploded.

Gyms transformed from niche training spaces into cultural landmarks.

In places like Venice Beach, California, muscular physiques became symbols of ambition and discipline. Movies amplified everything.

Suddenly muscles weren’t merely functional. They were cinematic.

The rise of Arnold Schwarzenegger changed the public image of gyms forever.

Fitness became aspirational entertainment.

At the same time, aerobics surged in popularity. The 1980s turned exercise into music, choreography, color, energy, and community.

Living rooms became mini fitness studios. Spandex became unavoidable.

Joseph insists humanity peaked culturally somewhere between cassette tapes and leg warmers.

I’m still undecided.

But what mattered most was this: exercise had fully entered mainstream life.

Not just for athletes.

For everyone.

Office workers. Parents. Teenagers. Retirees.

The modern gym industry was born.

The 20th century: Muscles meet media

"Exercise is King."
"Nutrition is Queen"
"Add the two and you have a Kingdom" - Jack LaLanne

Today: Data, Devices & Digital Discipline

Fast forward to today.

Fitness has become deeply technological.

Our watches monitor heart rates. Phones count steps. Apps analyze sleep. Algorithms generate workouts. AI coaches critique technique.

Entire gyms now exist inside subscriptions.

You can train with a virtual instructor in Tokyo while standing barefoot in your garage in Welkom.

That would sound like science fiction to Hippolyte Triat.💭

Yet despite all the innovation, the fundamentals barely changed.

  • Push
  • Pull
  • Squat
  • Carry
  • Jump
  • Run
  • Breathe

That’s still the foundation.

Strip away the LED screens and influencer hashtags, and modern fitness suddenly looks ancient again.

Humans still crave challenge.
Still seek transformation.
Still want confidence.
Still want energy.
Still want meaning.

Only the packaging evolved.

Chris glanced around Snow Haven while Joseph explained this.

Chris: "So basically the Greeks had outdoor CrossFit?"
Joseph: "Without posting about it afterward."

That may honestly be the biggest historical difference of all.

What This Means for Us

At some point the conversation drifted quietly.

The afternoon crowd at Snow Haven thickened. Plates clattered. Somewhere outside, a motorcycle roared past.

Joseph tapped the table thoughtfully.

Joseph: "You know what’s funny? We think fitness is about building bodies."

He shook his head.

Joseph: "Historically, it was always about building people."

That changes how you see gyms.

Because throughout history,movement has represented far more than appearance.

    It represented:
  • readiness
  • Discipline.
  • Confidence.
  • Health.
  • Belonging.
  • Resilience.
  • Sometimes even hope.

Think about it.

Why does somebody begin exercising after heartbreak?
After illness?
After burnout?
After loss?

Because movement feels like reclaiming control.

The ancients understood that. Even if they used different language.

And perhaps that explains why gyms continue evolving.

Not because humans changed.

Because human struggles didn’t.

Every generation searches for strength.

Physical. Mental. Emotional. Social.

And somehow, movement keeps becoming part of the answer.

Where We Go Next

The story, however, is far from finished.

Because gyms would eventually become something the ancient Greeks never imagined:

Brands.

Entire lifestyles emerged around fitness. Commercial chains exploded across cities. Supplements became billion-dollar industries. Social media transformed workouts into content.

Somewhere along the way, the gym stopped being merely a place.

It became culture.

In the next installment of The History of Gyms, we’ll explore how bodybuilding dungeons evolved into luxury wellness clubs, why modern fitness marketing became so powerful, and whether the future of gyms is becoming more human… or less.

Because the deeper we dig into fitness history, the clearer one thing becomes:

This was never really about dumbbells.

It was always about us.
