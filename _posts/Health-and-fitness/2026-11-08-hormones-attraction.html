The Scene: UNISA, South Africa — Lunchtime

Four Human Sciences students. One looming assignment. Two hamburgers, a chicken burger, chips, two cold drinks,a strawberry and a chocolate milkshake. And a question that could make their next tutorial very awkward.

At a busy student canteen at the University of South Africa, four classmates commandeered a table beside a window. Their assignment asked: How do testosterone and estrogen influence attraction between women and men — and could modern lifestyle choices change the story? The conversation below is fictional; the research it discusses is real.

  • Lerato — a Sesotho-speaking psychology student who questions easy explanations about human behaviour.
  • Thabo — a Sesotho-speaking Human Sciences student, keen on football and exercise, but suspicious of gym myths.
  • Chantel — an Afrikaans-speaking student of Dutch ancestry, interested in development, health and relationships.
  • Jan — an Afrikaans-speaking student of Dutch ancestry, a gaming enthusiast who likes statistics and occasionally forgets to sleep.
Jan: “Our lecturer wants hormones, attraction, evolution and lifestyle. Is there any chance we can submit a meme?”
Lerato: “Only if the meme has peer-reviewed references.”

Three Different Questions Hiding in One

Chantel: “Before we start, can we separate the things people keep mixing together?”

She opened her notebook and wrote three headings:

  1. Appearance: hormones influence physical development — muscle, voice, skin, fat distribution and secondary sexual characteristics.
  2. Desire: hormones participate in sexual motivation and libido in women and men.
  3. Preference: which particular person we find attractive is a much more complicated question, and current hormone levels do not reliably predict it.
Thabo: “So testosterone can help build the body people see, without being a secret attraction signal?”
Lerato: “Exactly. A face is not a blood test. And wanting sex is not the same as wanting a particular partner.”

The Ancient Question: Why Might Strength Have Mattered?

Thabo reached for a chip. “Imagine a much older world, without supermarkets, security companies or delivery apps.”

Thabo: “If someone could defend you, help find food and compete for scarce resources, that might have mattered to family survival.”
Chantel: “Three proposed benefits, then: protection, provisioning — including hunting and food acquisition — and resource competition, meaning the ability to secure or defend access to resources.”
Lerato: “Good evolutionary hypotheses. But do not write that every woman in history chose the strongest man. Human societies also depended on cooperation, social bonds, skill, caregiving and shared resources. And we cannot interview people who lived thousands of years ago.”

These explanations appear in evolutionary psychology as possible reasons that visible strength became attractive in some contexts. They are hypotheses about evolutionary history, not proof that every woman seeks a protector or that physical strength automatically makes someone a better provider today.

Jan: “In 2026, being able to carry a sofa is useful. So is being able to pay rent.”

The 70% Rule — And What It Really Means

Chantel: “Here is the headline from the podcast. In a 2017 study, how strong a man looked explained roughly 70% of the variation in women’s ratings of male bodily attractiveness. Adding height and leanness brought the model to about 80%.”
Jan: “Seventy percent of attraction? That is enormous!”
Lerato: “No. Seventy percent of the variation in body-attractiveness ratings in that study. Not 70% of love, dating success or marriage. And it does not mean 70% of women prefer a bodybuilder.”

Sell and colleagues studied perceived upper-body strength and bodily attractiveness. Stronger-looking bodies tended to receive higher ratings within their samples. The finding concerns visual bodily attractiveness under controlled conditions; it does not establish that changing testosterone makes a person more attractive, nor does it explain kindness, compatibility or affection. [1]

Thabo: “That is actually encouraging. Strength can be developed over time — unlike the jawbone I inherited from my grandfather.”

Testosterone: Builder, Not a Magic Magnet

During male puberty, testosterone helps deepen the voice, increase muscle mass, promote facial and body hair, and shape aspects of sexual development. Genetics, nutrition, timing of puberty, training and environment also influence the final appearance. A muscular adult may have ordinary testosterone levels and years of consistent training.

Jan: “So if two men have different testosterone blood results, women cannot just see the numbers?”
Chantel: “Correct. The Pound study found that masculine facial structure did not simply track baseline testosterone. Men with more masculine faces did show a greater testosterone response after winning a competition — but that is not proof that the face predicts testosterone all day, or that winning makes a man desirable.”

Pound and colleagues examined facial masculinity, baseline testosterone and competition-related hormone responses. Their result is interesting precisely because it separates resting hormone concentrations from short-term hormonal responses to experience. [2]

Thabo: “That is where people say girls like sports boys because winning increases testosterone.”
Lerato: “Careful. That study did not test whether girls preferred those boys. Sport might affect appearance, social confidence, friendships, skill or status. Those are possible pathways, but we would need different studies to establish which ones actually explain attraction.”

Muscle Is Built Over Years, Not in One Hormone Spike

A training session can produce short-lived changes in circulating hormones, but long-term muscular development depends on repeated training stimulus, sufficient nutrition, recovery, genetics and time. The hormone response to winning a match is not a shortcut to muscularity.

Thabo: “A person who plays football, swims, dances or lifts weights may become fitter and stronger. That can change posture, body composition and how comfortable they feel in their own skin.”
Chantel: “And there is another useful distinction: strength training can improve health whether or not someone’s appearance changes, and whether or not another person finds them attractive.”

A 2022 meta-analysis in eLife reported that strength and muscularity were more consistent correlates of mating and reproductive outcomes than facial masculinity among the masculine traits assessed. These are population-level associations, not promises for an individual. [3]

Puberty: What Can Lifestyle Change — and What Can It Not?

Jan: “Our assignment asks about young people. If you grow up eating well and playing sport, do you get more testosterone and become more attractive later?”
Lerato: “That sentence jumps across three links without testing any of them. We should separate normal puberty, health and adult attraction.”

Puberty is regulated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis and influenced by genetics, age, energy availability and health. Sufficient energy, protein and micronutrients support normal growth. Severe undernutrition, major illness or excessive energy deficit can interfere with maturation. Obesity and metabolic health can also interact with reproductive hormones, although patterns differ by sex, age and developmental stage.

Regular activity supports bone, muscle, cardiometabolic health and mental well-being. But no single sport, food or workout guarantees higher testosterone, a more masculine face, higher estrogen or better romantic prospects. Development is not a beauty competition.

Chantel: “And for girls, estrogen supports breast development, changes in fat distribution and skeletal maturation. For boys, estrogen also matters — some testosterone is converted to estradiol. Neither hormone belongs exclusively to one sex.”

Fast Food, Takeaways and the Body That Develops

Jan pushed his burger aside and looked offended. “Are you about to tell me lunch is ruining my future love life?”

Chantel: “No. One hamburger is a meal, not a destiny. The concern is an overall pattern: frequent energy-dense, nutrient-poor food, too little variety, and inadequate sleep or activity.”

High consumption of ultra-processed foods has been associated with greater odds of overweight or obesity among adolescents in observational syntheses. That does not prove that a particular takeaway directly suppresses testosterone or changes future attractiveness. Food processing, overall dietary quality, energy intake, socioeconomic factors and activity patterns can overlap. [4]

Thabo: “So the practical question is whether a young person has enough nourishment and activity to develop well, not whether they ate chips on Friday.”
Lerato: “Exactly. A nutritious pattern can include affordable local foods: beans, eggs, maize, vegetables, fruit, dairy where suitable, and other sources of protein. The aim is adequacy and variety, not expensive supplements.”

Gaming, Screens and Sitting for Hours

Jan: “I feel this section is targeting me personally.”
Lerato: “Gaming itself does not automatically lower testosterone. The issue is what gets displaced if it takes over the day: movement, sleep, meals, outdoor time or face-to-face contact.”

WHO guidelines advise children and adolescents aged 5–17 to average at least 60 minutes per day of moderate-to-vigorous activity across the week, with muscle- and bone-strengthening activities on at least three days. They also recommend limiting sedentary time, especially recreational screen time. For adults, the guideline is 150–300 minutes of moderate activity weekly, or an equivalent vigorous amount, plus muscle strengthening on at least two days. [5]

Chantel: “If someone games for five hours but also sleeps well, exercises, studies and maintains friendships, that is different from someone who sits all evening and sleeps four hours.”
Thabo: “And gaming can build friendships and teamwork. We should not pretend every screen is harmful or every athlete is healthy.”

The Sleep Question — More Complicated Than a Headline

Jan: “Fine. What about my habit of gaming until two in the morning?”
Chantel: “Sleep matters to mood, recovery, learning and overall health. A small 2011 study of ten young men found lower daytime testosterone after a week of five-hour nights. But later randomized studies have not all reproduced that effect.”

Leproult and Van Cauter reported a roughly 10–15% reduction in daytime testosterone under a short, severe sleep-restriction protocol. A later randomized investigation of acute and longer-term sleep restriction did not find a consistent adverse testosterone effect. These results should not be flattened into “one late night destroys testosterone.” [6] [7]

Lerato: “Even without a guaranteed hormone effect, losing sleep can make it harder to train, concentrate, regulate emotions and connect with other people. Those are worthwhile reasons to protect it.”

Estrogen: Can Men See It in Women?

Thabo: “We have spent ages on men. What about estrogen and female attractiveness?”
Chantel: “Estrogen helps shape some features associated with female development — including breast tissue, fat distribution and aspects of skin physiology. A 2006 study found that certain estrogen measurements were associated with ratings of facial femininity, health and attractiveness.”

Law Smith and colleagues examined reproductive hormone measures and facial appearance. Their findings were correlational: they did not show that increasing estrogen would make someone more attractive, or that a man can estimate a woman’s estradiol level from her face. [8]

Jan: “So estrogen is not a beauty slider either?”
Lerato: “Exactly. Skin, body shape and appearance also reflect genes, age, health, environment and culture. Beauty ideals change. People differ. And no one owes the world a particular appearance.”

Estrogen-responsive tissues include skin and connective tissue. Menopausal hormonal changes can affect skin thickness, hydration and elasticity, but clinical hormone therapy decisions depend on symptoms and medical risk, not on trying to achieve a beauty ideal.

Plot Twist: Men Need Estrogen, and Women Need Testosterone

Chantel: “Men convert some testosterone into estradiol through aromatase. Estradiol participates in male sexual and reproductive physiology. Women also produce testosterone, which contributes to sexual physiology.”
Jan: “So the question “Does estrogen make men attracted to women?” has two meanings.”
Lerato: “Right. A man’s own estrogen may participate in sexual desire. A woman’s estrogen may help shape visible features. Neither finding proves that he detects her estrogen or that higher estrogen makes her universally more attractive.”

Similarly, testosterone contributes to male development and to sexual function in both sexes, but desire, arousal, attraction to an individual and relationship choice are not interchangeable. Hormone treatment is a medical matter, not a dating strategy.

Ovulation: Do Women Suddenly Prefer More Masculine Men?

Thabo: “I have heard that women prefer stronger jaws or deeper voices when they ovulate.”
Chantel: “It is a famous hypothesis, but larger studies with repeated hormone measurements have struggled to support predictable shifts in facial masculinity preferences.”

Jones and colleagues followed women across repeated sessions and found no compelling evidence that changes in hormonal status reliably tracked preferences for male facial masculinity. Hormones can influence sexual motivation without necessarily changing which kind of person someone prefers. [9]

Lerato: “There is a difference between feeling more interested in intimacy and suddenly deciding a particular jawline is irresistible.”
Jan: “So our assignment should not say that women become attracted to “alpha males” every month.”
Everyone: “Definitely not.”

The Sports Boy, the Gamer and the Problem with Stereotypes

The canteen had begun to empty. A group of students passed carrying football boots; another table was debating a multiplayer game tournament.

Thabo: “What if someone is sporty, visibly fit and confident? That combination could make an impression.”
Lerato: “Yes, it could. But do not attribute the whole impression to testosterone. Fitness, social opportunities, confidence, grooming, shared interests and personality are different possible influences.”
Jan: “And what if the gamer is thoughtful, funny, kind, sleeps properly and goes hiking on weekends?”
Chantel: “Then he is a person, not a stereotype. Being sporty does not guarantee kindness, and gaming does not prevent attractiveness. We must not turn a statistical finding into a ranking of human worth.”

The most plausible lifestyle-to-attraction pathway is indirect: habits influence health, strength, energy, mood, opportunities to meet people and sometimes appearance. Those factors can influence social experiences. But longitudinal evidence proving that childhood takeaway meals or gaming hours directly cause lower adult romantic attractiveness is lacking.

The Four Students Write Their Conclusion

Lerato: “Hormones shape some of what we see. They also participate in desire. But they do not tell us whom we will love.”
Thabo: “Strength may have been useful in ancestral environments for protection, provisioning and resource competition. Today it can still be visually attractive, and it is trainable — but it is not the whole of attraction.”
Chantel: “Estrogen and testosterone both matter in both sexes. Appearance cannot reveal precise hormone levels. Men and women are not controlled by a simple chemical switch.”
Jan: “And modern habits matter to health and development. We should eat reasonably, move often, sleep well and keep gaming in perspective — not because it guarantees dates, but because it gives us a better foundation for life.”

They agreed on the sentence that would end their assignment:
“Biology helps write the opening chapter of attraction;
experience, relationships and individual choice write much of the rest.”

Practical Takeaways for Young Adults

  • Move regularly: choose enjoyable sport, walking, dancing, cycling or resistance training; consistency matters more than a particular sport.
  • Build strength gradually: progressive, age-appropriate training and recovery support fitness and function.
  • Eat for growth and health: prioritize adequate energy, protein and dietary variety; a takeaway is not a hormonal disaster.
  • Protect sleep: regular, sufficient sleep supports health even though testosterone findings are mixed.
  • Balance screens: gaming is not inherently unhealthy; watch what excessive sitting or late-night play displaces.
  • Do not chase hormone numbers for attractiveness: supplements or hormone medication are not proven shortcuts to desirability.
  • Value the whole person: mutual respect, humour, trust, shared interests and compatibility matter beyond appearance.

Final Thought: The Last Milkshake

Jan lifted the last of his milkshake. “So the secret to attraction is not a laboratory number?”

Lerato: “It never was.”
Thabo: “But I am still going to football practice.”
Chantel: “And Jan is still going to game tonight.”
Jan: “Yes. But this time I am logging off before midnight.”

They laughed, packed their notes and headed back to class. Their assignment was not about becoming the most attractive people on campus. It was about understanding how human biology, behaviour and culture meet — sometimes predictably, sometimes surprisingly, and always with more complexity than a single hormone.
